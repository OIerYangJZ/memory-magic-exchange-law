Popular Summary (PRX Quantum) -- DRAFT, to be revised by the authors

A fault-tolerant quantum computer pays for most of its work in "magic states", the costly resource consumed by every T gate. Many algorithms, such as Trotterized simulation, randomized compiling and layered variational circuits, hand the computer a rotation angle in several pieces that arrive in different rounds. The machine then has a choice. It can hold the pieces in classical memory until the whole angle is known and synthesize one rotation, or it can execute each piece on arrival and spend magic states before it knows the final answer.

We show that these two costs, bits of deferred classical state and committed magic states, can be converted into each other at a fixed exchange rate that does not depend on the size of the problem. For the standard single-qubit Clifford+T synthesis pipelines, every bit of memory a schedule gives up costs at least two committed T gates. This is a rigorous bound that rests on a new count of how many cheap gate sequences lie near a rotation. It holds even when the pieces cancel and the final operation is the identity. An explicit scheme achieves three T gates per bit. Exhaustive enumeration of Clifford+T words, together with a stated conjecture, indicates that three is the true rate and that memory should be given up in whole rotations rather than bit by bit. We also show how side information, probabilistic mixing and measurement-based synthesis change the rate.

The results tell compiler designers how to price classical memory against magic-state consumption.
